# 1.2 Estimating Action Values

## Learning Objectives
- Understand what an **action value** is and why we need to estimate it.
- Learn the **sample-average method** for estimating action values from experience.
- Recognize the tension between **exploitation** (greedy) and **exploration**.

## Action Value: Definition

The **action value** `q(a)` of an action `a` is the **expected reward** the agent receives when it takes that action in a given state:

```
q(a) = E[ R_t | A_t = a ]
```

In other words: "If I always pick action `a`, what's the average reward I should expect?"

The catch: the agent does **not know** the true `q(a)` in advance. It only observes rewards *after* taking the action. So the agent must **estimate** the action value from experience and use that estimate to make decisions.

**Real-life analogy.** Imagine picking a coffee shop on campus. The "true value" of shop A is the average taste + price + wait you would get over hundreds of visits. You've only visited it 3 times — so you work with an *estimate* based on those 3 experiences, and you refine it after every new visit.

## Sample-Average Method

The simplest way to estimate `q(a)` is the **sample-average estimator**: sum all rewards you've ever received from taking action `a`, then divide by the number of times you've taken it.

```
Q_n(a) = (R_1 + R_2 + ... + R_n) / n
```

Where:
- `Q_n(a)` is your **estimate** of `q(a)` after `n` selections of action `a`.
- `R_i` is the reward observed on the `i`-th time you took action `a`.

By the **law of large numbers**, as `n → ∞`, the sample average converges to the true expected value: `Q_n(a) → q(a)`.

### Worked Example: Clinical Trials

A doctor has **k = 3 treatments** (call them P, Q, R). Reward is `+1` if the patient improves, `0` otherwise. Start with all estimates at `0`.

| Step | Action chosen | Reward observed | Updated Q(a) |
|------|---------------|-----------------|--------------|
| 1    | P             | 1               | Q(P) = 1/1 = 1.0 |
| 2    | P             | 0               | Q(P) = (1+0)/2 = 0.5 |
| 3    | Q             | 1               | Q(Q) = 1/1 = 1.0 |
| ...  | ...           | ...             | ... |

After enough trials, each `Q(a)` becomes a reliable proxy for the true success rate of treatment `a`.

**Analogy — your exam average.** Your current course grade is literally a sample average of all your test scores so far. After the midterm (n=1) one bad score hurts a lot; after the final (n=5) that one score barely moves the needle. Same idea: the *variance* of the estimate shrinks as `n` grows.

## Incremental Update Rule

You don't need to store every past reward. The sample average can be written recursively, which is hugely useful in practice (and the form you'll see throughout the RL textbook):

```
Q_{n+1} = Q_n + (1/n) * (R_n - Q_n)
```

Read it as: **new estimate = old estimate + step size × (target − old estimate)**.

- `R_n − Q_n` is the **prediction error** — how wrong your current estimate was on this trial.
- `1/n` is the step size; it shrinks over time so old estimates aren't overwritten too quickly.

### Why this matters
- **Constant memory**: O(1) per action, no need to remember all past rewards.
- **Online learning**: update the estimate the instant the new reward arrives.
- **Same form generalizes**: later in the course you'll replace `1/n` with a constant `α` (constant step size), which lets the agent adapt to *non-stationary* problems where the true `q(a)` drifts over time.

## Greedy Action and the Exploit–Explore Tradeoff

Once you have estimates `Q(a)` for every action, you can act on them.

**Greedy action**: the action with the **largest current estimated value**.

```
a* = argmax_a Q(a)
```

- **Exploitation**: always picking the greedy action. You milk what you already know is good.
- **Exploration**: picking a non-greedy action on purpose. You sacrifice some immediate reward to gather information that might reveal an even better action later.

**Why not just be greedy forever?** If you tried treatment P twice and it worked once (Q(P) = 0.5), but you never tried Q or R, you might be missing a treatment that works 90% of the time. Greedy = "stuck in a mediocre coffee shop forever because the first one you tried was OK." Exploration = "wander one block further and maybe find a gem."

**Bias–variance flavor (intuition).** Early on (small `n`), your `Q(a)` estimates are noisy — high variance, and any single bad trial shifts them a lot. Greedy choices at this stage are basically biased toward whichever action happened to be tried first. As `n` grows, variance drops, estimates become reliable, and the greedy action reliably tracks the truly best action.

## Key Takeaways
- The **action value** `q(a)` is the expected reward of taking action `a`; since it's unknown, the agent learns an **estimate** `Q(a)`.
- The **sample-average estimator** `Q_n = (R_1 + ... + R_n)/n` converges to `q(a)` by the law of large numbers.
- The equivalent **incremental update** `Q_{n+1} = Q_n + (1/n)(R_n − Q_n)` lets you update estimates online with O(1) memory per action.
- The **greedy action** maximizes the current estimate; **exploitation** uses that knowledge, **exploration** deliberately tries non-greedy actions to learn more.
- Early estimates are high-variance, so early greedy choices can be misleading — this is the core motivation for balancing exploration with exploitation (formalized in upcoming ε-greedy and UCB methods).
- Sample averages with shrinking step size `1/n` are great for **stationary** problems; constant step sizes are the natural extension to non-stationary ones.
