# 2.8 Off-policy Temporal Difference Control: Q-Learning

## What is Q-Learning?

**Q-Learning** is a model-free, **off-policy** Reinforcement Learning algorithm that learns the **optimal action-value function** `Q*(s, a)` — the expected cumulative reward starting from state `s`, taking action `a`, and then following the *optimal* policy thereafter.

The key idea is simple but powerful: while the agent behaves using some exploratory policy (often ε-greedy), the **target** it learns about is always the **optimal** policy. This is what makes it *off-policy*: the behavior policy and the target policy are different.

### Analogy: Learn from watching a clumsy friend
Imagine you and your friend are playing a maze game. Your friend keeps bumping into walls (an exploratory, sometimes-random policy). You don't copy them — instead, after each move, you ask yourself, "If I had played the *best possible* next move instead, how good would that have been?" Over many of your friend's clumsy attempts, you still figure out the optimal path. **That's Q-learning.** Your friend's behavior generates the data; your target is the best play.

---

## The Q-Learning Update Rule

The heart of Q-learning is the action-value update:

```
Q(S, A) ← Q(S, A) + α · [ R + γ · max_a Q(S', a) − Q(S, A) ]
```

Where:
- `S, A` = current state and action taken
- `R` = observed reward after taking action `A` in state `S`
- `S'` = next state
- `α` = learning rate (0 < α ≤ 1)
- `γ` = discount factor (0 ≤ γ < 1)
- `max_a Q(S', a)` = **best** next-state action value (not the one actually taken)

### How to read it
The expression in brackets is the **TD error**: `[target − old estimate]`. The target `R + γ · max_a Q(S', a)` is what `Q(S, A)` *should* be (assuming optimal play from now on). We nudge our current estimate toward that target by a step size `α`.

Because the update uses `max` (not the action actually executed), the algorithm directly solves the **Bellman optimality equation** for action values, sidestepping the alternating "policy evaluation → policy improvement" cycle of classical dynamic programming.

---

## Why It's Off-Policy

Two policies are at play:

| Policy | Role | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Behavior policy** | Generates the data (what the agent *does*) | ε-greedy over current Q |
| **Target policy** | What the agent *learns about* | Greedy (argmax over Q) |

Because they differ, Q-learning is **off-policy**. SARSA, in contrast, uses the same policy for both — it is **on-policy**.

### Practical consequence
You can reuse data collected by *any* policy (including older policies or even human demonstrations) and still converge to the optimal policy, as long as the behavior policy keeps visiting all state-action pairs infinitely often (a coverage / GLIE-style assumption).

---

## Q-Learning vs SARSA

| Aspect | **SARSA** (On-policy) | **Q-Learning** (Off-policy) |
|---|---|---|
| Update target | `R + γ · Q(S', A')` — uses action actually taken next | `R + γ · max_a Q(S', a)` — assumes optimal next move |
| Policy learned | Value of the *current* behavior policy | Value of the *optimal* policy |
| Action selection | Follows policy being evaluated | Uses exploratory policy |
| Convergence | ε-optimal w.r.t. the current policy | Converges to `Q*(s, a)` and thus `π*` |
| Safety in risky environments | Safer — learns consequences of its own exploratory moves | Riskier — may chase rewards through dangerous exploratory paths |

**Mnemonic:** SARSA uses the quintuple `(S, A, R, S', A')` — both the next-state *and* the next-action. Q-learning only needs `(S, A, R, S')` because it assumes you'd pick the best `A'` anyway.

### Joke: The optimist and the realist
- **Q-learning** is the eternal optimist: "Next time I'll *definitely* take the best move."
- **SARSA** is the cautious realist: "Next time I'll probably do what I just did."

Both are valid; they just describe different things.

## Q-Learning Algorithm (Step-by-Step)

1. **Initialize** the Q-table (e.g., zeros) for all states and actions.
2. **Choose an action** `A` from state `S` using an exploratory policy (e.g., ε-greedy).
3. **Perform the action** and observe reward `R` and next state `S'`.
4. **Update the Q-table** using the rule above.
5. **Repeat** steps 2–4 for many episodes.

After enough iterations, the Q-table approximates `Q*(s, a)`, and the greedy policy derived from it (`π*(s) = argmax_a Q(s, a)`) is optimal.

---

## Worked Example: Frozen Lake

In the classic **frozen lake** grid, the agent must cross from start to goal without falling into holes.

- The Q-table maps each cell (state) to a value for each of the four actions (up/down/left/right).
- Over many episodes, `Q(s, a)` grows toward the expected return for taking action `a` in state `s`.
- A greedy walk through the highest Q-values traces the **shortest safe path** to the goal.

This example makes the abstract idea concrete: the Q-table *is* the agent's learned knowledge, and updating it is how that knowledge improves.

---

## Convergence Conditions

Q-learning converges to the optimal action-value function `Q*` if:

1. All state-action pairs are visited **infinitely often** (sufficient exploration).
2. The learning rate `α` decays appropriately (e.g., Robbins–Monro: `Σ α = ∞`, `Σ α² < ∞`).
3. Rewards and dynamics are bounded (finite MDP).
4. `γ ∈ [0, 1)`.

Under these conditions, Q-learning is provably optimal — independent of the policy generating the data.

## Applications

Q-learning is widely used in:
- **Robotics** — learning manipulation and locomotion policies
- **Game playing** — Atari, board games
- **Autonomous systems** — navigation, resource management
- **Recommendation systems** — sequential decision-making

---

## Key Takeaways

- **Q-learning is off-policy**: it learns the value of the *optimal* policy while following an *exploratory* behavior policy.
- The **update rule** is `Q(S,A) ← Q(S,A) + α[R + γ·max_a Q(S',a) − Q(S,A)]`, where the `max` is the off-policy signature.
- Unlike SARSA, Q-learning uses the **best possible next action**, not the action actually taken — that's why it's "optimistic."
- Q-learning **directly solves the Bellman optimality equation** using samples, without alternating evaluation and improvement steps.
- It **converges to `Q*`** (and therefore the optimal policy `π*`) given enough exploration and decaying step sizes.
- Compared with SARSA, it is **riskier in dangerous environments** but **more direct** when you only care about optimal performance.
- The **Q-table** is the agent's knowledge — every update is one tiny step toward the optimal decision at that state.
