# 2.3 Exploration Methods for Monte-Carlo

## The Problem with Exploring Starts

The previous lecture introduced **Monte-Carlo Control with Exploring Starts**, which requires that every state-action pair `(s, a)` has a non-zero probability of being selected as the starting point of an episode. This guarantees the algorithm sees every pair and converges to the optimal policy.

In practice, however, this assumption is often unrealistic or impossible:

- **Sampling a random initial state-action pair may be infeasible.** Consider a self-driving car: to "explore starts," you would have to drop the car in every conceivable initial configuration — e.g. in the middle of a busy freeway, at varied speeds, in every weather condition. That is dangerous, expensive, and often illegal.
- Without exploring starts, the agent may under-visit certain regions of the state-action space and **converge to a suboptimal policy**.

We therefore need an alternative exploration mechanism that works directly from the agent's *normal* interaction with the environment.

> **Analogy:** Imagine trying every restaurant in a city. "Exploring starts" says teleport the taxi to a random neighborhood each evening — fun, but impractical. A better strategy is to mostly walk to your favorite spot but occasionally wander next door.

## ε-Greedy Policies (Quick Recap)

The textbook answer is borrowed from the **multi-armed bandit** literature: use an **ε-greedy policy**.

- With probability `1 - ε` take the **greedy** action (the one with the highest current action-value estimate).
- With probability `ε` take a **random** action.

These policies are **stochastic**: they specify a *probability distribution* over actions, not a single action. As a result, the agent follows a *slightly different trajectory* almost every episode, naturally exploring the environment while still exploiting what it has learned.

```
π(a | s) = 1 - ε + ε/|A(s)|   if a = argmax_a' Q(s, a')
π(a | s) = ε / |A(s)|         otherwise
```

## ε-Soft Policies

ε-greedy is actually a special case of a broader class called **ε-soft policies**.

**Definition.** A policy π is **ε-soft** if for every state `s` and every action `a ∈ A(s)`:

```
π(a | s) ≥ ε / |A(s)|
```

That is, *no action is ever completely ruled out* — every action retains at least a small floor probability of being chosen. A **deterministic policy** violates this (it assigns probability 0 to non-greedy actions); a truly random uniform policy is ε-soft with `ε = 1`.

ε-greedy policies sit in the middle: they concentrate mass on the greedy action while keeping a uniform tail of `ε / |A(s)|` over the rest — guaranteeing the ε-soft property automatically.

### Why This Matters for Monte-Carlo Control

By restricting the policy-improvement step to the set of **ε-soft policies**, we get a useful theoretical guarantee (from Sutton & Barto):

- **Policy Improvement Theorem for ε-soft policies:** Updating Q toward the greedy action *within* the ε-soft class strictly improves the policy's value (or leaves it unchanged).
- Combined with **GLIE** (Greedy in the Limit with Infinite Exploration — covered in detail later), this lets Monte-Carlo control converge to the **optimal ε-soft policy** — the best policy you can achieve while still exploring.

> **Joke:** A deterministic policy is a stubborn foodie who always orders the same dish. An ε-soft policy is that same foodie, but with one rule: *"always try a new dish at least once every few meals."* You still mostly eat your favorite, but you keep discovering better options.

## Visualizing the Difference

The lecture's grid-world illustration makes the contrast vivid:

- **Deterministic policy:** one arrow out of every cell. The agent traces the *exact same path* from the start state every episode.
- **ε-greedy policy:** every cell shows the *same dominant arrow* (the greedy action) but technically has a small probability of taking any other action. Episode trajectories vary, so the agent eventually samples state-action pairs it would never have visited under the deterministic policy.

## From Exploring Starts to On-Policy ε-Soft Control

Putting the pieces together, the **on-policy Monte-Carlo control with ε-soft policies** loop looks like:

1. Initialize `Q(s, a)` arbitrarily and set a small `ε > 0`.
2. Generate an episode using the current **ε-soft** policy.
3. For each state-action pair `(S_t, A_t)` in the episode, update `Q(S_t, A_t)` toward the observed return `G_t` (first-visit or every-visit MC).
4. Improve the policy by making it *more greedy* with respect to the updated Q, while still respecting the ε-soft constraint (i.e. the new policy is ε-greedy).
5. Repeat.

Because step 4 never assigns probability 0 to any action, the agent keeps exploring forever, and the policy monotonically improves.

## Key Takeaways

- **Exploring starts** is a clean theoretical trick but often impossible in real problems (self-driving car, robotics, healthcare).
- **ε-greedy policies** are stochastic: greedy most of the time, random with probability `ε`, ensuring continual exploration.
- **ε-soft policies** generalize ε-greedy — they require `π(a|s) ≥ ε / |A(s)|` for every action, so no action is ever completely starved of samples.
- Restricting improvement to **ε-soft policies** preserves the policy-improvement guarantee, letting on-policy MC control converge without exploring starts.
- Trajectories under stochastic policies differ every episode, naturally covering more of the state-action space than any deterministic policy could.
- In the next lecture, **GLIE** conditions tighten ε over time to recover the *optimal* (not just optimal ε-soft) policy.
- Practical rule of thumb: keep `ε` small but non-zero (e.g. `0.1`), and decay it slowly during training.
