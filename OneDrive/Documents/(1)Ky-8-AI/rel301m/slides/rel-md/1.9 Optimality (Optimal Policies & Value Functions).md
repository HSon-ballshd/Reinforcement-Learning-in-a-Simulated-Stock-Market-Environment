# 1.9 Optimality: Optimal Policies & Value Functions

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lecture you should be able to:
- **Define an optimal policy** (π*) and explain why it matters in RL.
- **Define the optimal state-value function** (v*) and **optimal action-value function** (q*).
- **Use v\* to recover an optimal policy** via the argmax relationship.
- Understand the **Bellman optimality equations** that characterize v* and q*.

---

## Why Policies Matter

A **policy** π is the agent's rulebook: it maps states (or state–action histories) to actions. Every concept in RL — returns, value functions, the Bellman equation — is defined *with respect to a policy*. So picking a "good" policy is the entire game.

Policies play several roles:
- **Action selection** — decides what the agent actually does in each state.
- **Learning objective** — defines what "good" means (maximize expected return under π).
- **Exploration vs. exploitation** — π balances trying new things vs. sticking with what's known to work.
- **Implicit evaluation** — encodes preferences over states/actions through expected rewards.
- **Adaptation** — lets the agent respond when the environment (or its model) changes.
- **Generalization** — a good π generalizes across similar states, not just memorized ones.
- **Knowledge representation** — in some algorithms (e.g., actor–critic, policy gradients), π *is* the learned knowledge.
- **Iterative improvement** — policy iteration, actor–critic, etc., update π step by step.

### Policy Taxonomy
- **Deterministic policy** π(s): one specific action per state.
- **Stochastic policy** π(a|s): a probability distribution over actions.
- **Optimal policy** π*: the one that maximizes expected cumulative reward.

---

## Defining an Optimal Policy

An **optimal policy** π* is the policy that **maximizes the expected cumulative reward** (i.e., the return) over time. It is, by definition, *at least as good as every other policy*:

```
π* ≥ π   for all policies π
```

Formally: for every state s, the expected return when starting from s and following π* is ≥ the expected return when following any other π from s.

### Real-life analogy
Think of π* as the **"perfect strategy"** in chess. You might know many legal ways to play, but π* is the one no opponent can beat in expectation. There can be more than one optimal policy (ties!), but every optimal policy shares the **same optimal value function**.

### How to find π*
In practice we don't enumerate policies — we use RL algorithms: **Q-learning**, **SARSA**, **policy-gradient methods**, **actor–critic**, **deep RL**, or direct methods like **value iteration** and **policy iteration**.

> **Caveat:** We can only *directly* solve small MDPs (e.g., via linear programming or exhaustive value iteration). For anything big we approximate.

---

## The Intuition: Two-Choice MDP

A classic teaching example. Imagine states X, Y, Z with rewards:

- From **X**: action **A1** → Y (reward r₁); action **A2** → Z (reward r₂).
- From **Y**: only **A1** takes you back to X.
- From **Z**: only **A1** takes you back to X.

Two deterministic candidate policies:
- **π₁**: always pick A1 → cycles X ↔ Y forever.
- **π₂**: always pick A2 → cycles X ↔ Z forever.

**Which is optimal?** Answer: **it depends on the discount factor γ**. If rewards are larger on the Y branch and you discount the future a lot, you may prefer π₂; if the Y branch pays off more in the long run, you prefer π₁. Optimality is *always relative to γ*.

## Optimal Value Functions

Once we have π*, we can define the value functions induced by it. Because π* is the best, these are the **best possible value functions**.

### Optimal state-value function v*

```
v*(s) = max_π v_π(s)   for all s ∈ S
```

It is the **maximum expected return** achievable starting from state s.

### Optimal action-value function q*

```
q*(s, a) = max_π q_π(s, a)
```

It is the **maximum expected return** achievable starting from s, taking action a, then behaving optimally afterwards.

### Connection between v* and q*

```
v*(s) = max_a q*(s, a)
q*(s, a) = E[ R_{t+1} + γ v*(S_{t+1}) | S_t = s, A_t = a ]
```

These two identities are the bridge from "values" back to "actions".

---

## Bellman Optimality Equations

The standard Bellman equation references a specific policy π. For π*, we can write a **special form that doesn't reference π at all** — only the max operator.

### Bellman optimality equation for v*

```
v*(s) = max_a  Σ_{s', r}  p(s', r | s, a) [ r + γ v*(s') ]
```

Read it as: *the value of being in s is the best action's expected one-step reward plus discounted optimal future value*.

### Bellman optimality equation for q*

```
q*(s, a) = Σ_{s', r}  p(s', r | s, a) [ r + γ  max_{a'} q*(s', a') ]
```

Here the agent has already committed to action a; afterwards it picks the best follow-up action a'.

### Why "no regret" framing?
Bellman optimality assumes **the agent will make the best choice at every future step**. That's why it's a `max` instead of an expectation over π(a'|s'). It's the definition of doing the right thing every time — no lookback, no regrets.

---

## Recovering π* from v* (and q*)

This is the crucial practical link.

```
π*(s) = argmax_a  Σ_{s', r} p(s', r | s, a) [ r + γ v*(s') ]
```

- `v*(s)` is the **value** of state s under optimal play.
- `π*(s)` is the **argmax** — the specific action that *achieves* that maximum.

So once you know v*, the optimal action at any state is the one with the highest one-step lookahead. The max gives you the value; the argmax gives you the policy. **Same information, two views.**

---

## Worked Example: Gridworld with γ = 0.9

Consider a small gridworld:
- From state **A**, every action leads to **A'** with reward **+10**.
- From state **B**, every action leads to **B'** with reward **+5**.
- All other transitions give reward **0**, except bumping into a wall gives **−1**.
- Discount factor γ = 0.9.

By symmetry within A and within B, every action looks the same locally — the optimal values are determined by the recurrence:

```
v*(A) = 10 + γ · v*(A')
v*(B) =  5 + γ · v*(B')
```

Solving gives v*(A) ≈ 88.9 (or whatever the recursive back-up yields depending on terminal values), with v*(A) > v*(B). When we then take the argmax over actions at each state, **any** action is fine inside A (they tie), but moving *toward A* dominates in the neighborhood of B.

> Takeaway: **value asymmetry between states → action asymmetry between states**. v\* tells you *how good* a state is; π\* tells you *which way to go*.

---

## Relationship Between Value Function and Optimal Policy

- The **value function** quantifies expected return from each state (or state–action pair).
- The **optimal policy** is the action-selection rule that achieves those maximum values.
- v* and π* are **two sides of the same coin**: knowing one lets you reconstruct the other (modulo ties).

In short: **v\* tells you the maximum reward you can hope for; π\* tells you how to get it.**

---

## Comparison Theorem for Optimal Policies (informal)

A deeper theoretical guarantee:

> Any policy π that is **greedy with respect to v*** is an **optimal policy**.

Greedy here means: at each state, pick the action that maximizes the one-step Bellman backup using v*. This theorem is what justifies **value iteration** and **policy iteration** — repeatedly making π greedy w.r.t. v improves (or maintains) the policy, and at the fixed point, π = π* and v = v*.

---

## Key Takeaways

- An **optimal policy π*** maximizes expected return from every state; it is ≥ every other policy.
- The **optimal state-value function v*(s)** is the maximum expected return from s; **q*(s, a)** is the max from (s, a).
- The **Bellman optimality equations** replace the policy expectation with a `max`, encoding "always choose the best future action."
- **π*(s) = argmax_a Σ p(s', r|s, a)[r + γ v*(s')]** — once you know v*, you can read off π*.
- **Optimality is relative to γ**: a policy optimal at γ = 0.5 may not be optimal at γ = 0.99.
- **Many MDPs admit multiple optimal policies** with the same v*, but they all share the same value function.
- **Small MDPs** can be solved directly (LP, exhaustive VI); for large MDPs we learn π* with Q-learning, policy gradients, or deep RL.
