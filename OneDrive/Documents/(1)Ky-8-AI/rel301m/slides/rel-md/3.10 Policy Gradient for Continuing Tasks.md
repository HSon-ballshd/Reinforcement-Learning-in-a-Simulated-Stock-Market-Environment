# Policy Gradient for Continuing Tasks

This deck shifts the policy-gradient story from **episodic** settings (games with a clear end) to **continuing** settings (tasks that just keep going forever). The discount factor gamma may or may not be 1, but the key trick is to replace the cumulative discounted return with a measure that makes sense when there is no natural "starting time" or "ending time".

## Continuing vs. Episodic Tasks

| Setting | Objective | Intuition |
|---|---|---|
| Episodic | Sum of rewards in a single episode | "How much score did I get this round?" |
| Continuing (discounted) | Discounted return G_t | Still useful, but mixes per-step reward with a baseline |
| Continuing (average reward) | Long-run average reward **r(π)** | "How well am I doing *right now* on average?" |

> **Analogy.** Running a 24/7 power plant never "ends". You don't measure success by a one-time profit — you measure it by the **average cost per megawatt-hour** sustained over weeks. That sustained rate is the **average reward**.

## The Objective for Continuing Tasks

The agent's goal is to **maximize the long-run average reward per time step**:

```
r(π) = Σ_s μ(s) · r(s,π(s))
```

Where:
- **μ(s)** is the **stationary distribution** — the fraction of time the Markov chain spends in state `s` when following policy `π`.
- **r(s, a)** is the expected immediate reward in state `s`.
- `r(π)` is sometimes written **r̄(π)** or **ρ(π)**.

**Key insight.** μ is *not* an arbitrary weighting — it is the distribution induced by letting the chain run forever under `π`. So `r(π)` answers: *given that I follow this policy indefinitely, what's my per-step reward rate?*

### Differential Return

Since the absolute level of return is meaningless in a continuing task (it grows without bound or stays at a constant offset), we use the **differential return**:

```
G_t = Σ_{k=0}^∞ (R_{t+k} − r(π))
```

That is: at each future step, ask **"how much better (or worse) than the average was this particular reward?"** The differential value function measures advantage over the long-run baseline.

> **Analogy.** A trader who always earns $100/day has G_t ≈ 0 every day — exactly average. A trader who pulls in $200 on a Tuesday has a "differential reward" of +$100 *for that Tuesday*. The variance above/below the mean is what matters.

## The Challenge: μ Depends on π

The hard part of the policy gradient in this setting is **the objective is a function of μ(π), and μ depends on the policy parameters θ**:

```
J(θ) = r(π_θ) = Σ_s μ_θ(s) · r_π(s)
```

If we change θ, we change the policy, which changes the stationary distribution, which changes the objective — *all at once*. This is why the proof of the policy-gradient theorem gets tricky.

## The Policy Gradient Theorem (Average-Reward Form)

The theorem says: even though the objective depends on μ in a complicated way, **the gradient has a clean sample-friendly form**:

```
∇J(θ) ∝ Σ_s d(s) · q(s, a) · ∇π(a | s, θ)
```

Where:
- **d(s)** — the **differential visitation** of state `s` (analogous to μ but weighted by time-since-visit; for ergodic chains it reduces to μ).
- **q(s, a)** — the **differential action-value**: expected future *differential* return starting from `(s, a)`.
- **∇π(a | s, θ)** — the gradient of the policy with respect to its parameters.

The proportional sign (∝) hides a constant factor. What matters is that **the gradient does not require differentiating the state distribution** — the chain-rule mess cancels out.

## On-Policy Update with a Baseline

In practice (REINFORCE-style updates for continuing tasks), we sample trajectories and push parameters up for actions whose differential returns beat a baseline:

```
θ ← θ + α · (G_t − b(s_t)) · ∇ln π(a_t | s_t, θ)
```

Where:
- **α** is the learning rate.
- **G_t** is the **sample differential return** (sum of `R_{t+k} − r̄`).
- **b(s_t)** is a **baseline** (e.g. a learned differential value `v̄(s_t)`) that reduces variance without introducing bias.
- `∇ln π(...)` is the **score function**.

> **Why a baseline helps.** Subtracting `b(s)` doesn't change the expected gradient (the extra term is zero in expectation) but it shrinks the variance — like comparing each day's profit to your *own average* instead of to zero.

## Putting It Together

1. **Run** the current policy for many steps in the environment.
2. **Estimate** `r(π)` empirically as the mean reward over a long window.
3. **Form** differential returns `G_t` using that running estimate.
4. **Update** θ with the REINFORCE-style rule above (or an actor-critic variant that learns `q(s,a)` or `v(s)`).
5. **Repeat** until `r(π)` plateaus.

This is essentially what algorithms like **average-reward REINFORCE**, **actor-critic with average reward**, and (in their discounted-reward cousins) **A2C / A3C / PPO** are doing internally.

## Real-World Examples

- **Data-center cooling.** Never "ends" — reward is energy saved per minute; baseline is the current average energy use.
- **Inventory replenishment.** A stock manager restocks shelves continuously; reward is profit minus holding cost per hour, baseline = long-run average profit.
- **Algorithmic trading.** A strategy runs continuously; reward = PnL per tick, differential reward = tick-PnL minus rolling mean PnL.

## Common Pitfalls

- **Estimating r(π) poorly.** If your running average is too noisy, differential returns are biased and learning is unstable.
- **Forgetting the baseline.** Pure REINFORCE without a baseline works but converges slowly.
- **Confusing discounted with average reward.** A γ close to 1 *approximates* average reward but is not identical; mixing them up skews gradients.
- **Episodic bias.** Using a small batch in a continuing task makes μ estimates wrong — let trajectories be long enough.

## Key Takeaways

- **Continuing tasks** have no natural start/end, so the right objective is the **long-run average reward** `r(π) = Σ μ(s)·r(s,π(s))`.
- The **differential return** `G_t = Σ (R_{t+k} − r(π))` measures how much *better than average* each reward is.
- The **stationary distribution μ** is induced by the policy itself — that's what makes the gradient theorem non-trivial.
- The **average-reward policy gradient theorem** gives the clean form `∇J(θ) ∝ Σ d(s)·q(s,a)·∇π(a|s,θ)`, which **does not require differentiating μ**.
- The on-policy update is `θ ← θ + α·(G_t − b(s))·∇ln π(a|s,θ)` — a baseline reduces variance without bias.
- This framework underpins average-reward REINFORCE and several actor-critic methods.
- Exam tip: when a question says "no episodes / no discount / 24-7 task", reach for **average reward + differential return**.
