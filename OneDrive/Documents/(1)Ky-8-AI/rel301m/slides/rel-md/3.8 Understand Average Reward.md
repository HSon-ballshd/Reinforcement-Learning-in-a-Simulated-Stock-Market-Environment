# 3.8 Understand Average Reward

## Motivation: Why Average Reward?

The **average reward formulation** matters when standard discounted RL feels awkward. Consider the running example in the slides: an agent on a ring with mostly no-choice states, but one special state `S` lets it pick **Left** or **Right**. Both paths eventually loop back to `S`.

- Left ring gives `+1` immediately after `S`.
- Right ring gives `+2` just before `S` (one step before returning).

The total reward per loop is the same in both directions. But with **discounting** (`γ < 1`), the timing changes everything:

- With small `γ` (myopic), the agent prefers **Left** because the `+1` reward arrives sooner.
- As `γ` grows toward 1, the agent eventually prefers **Right**.
- The break-even point is around **`γ ≈ 0.841`**.

Two practical problems emerge:

1. The "best" policy depends entirely on the discount you pick.
2. In continuing tasks, `γ = 1` is forbidden because returns can blow up to infinity.

**Average reward** sidesteps all of this — it measures the **rate** of reward per step, so timing within a cycle becomes irrelevant.

> **Analogy:** Think of average reward as your typical *hourly wage* instead of the *total paycheck*. Whether you got paid early Friday or late Sunday doesn't matter — only how much you earn per hour on average.

---

## The Average Reward Objective

### Definition

For a policy `π`, the **average reward** `r(π)` is the expected reward per time step in the long run:

```text
r(π) = lim_{T→∞}  E[ (1/T) * Σ_{t=1..T}  R_t ]
```

If `r(π)` exists (i.e., the Markov chain under `π` is ergodic), the policy's long-term performance is fully captured by this single scalar.

### Equivalent View via State Visitation

The same quantity can be written as an expectation over how often the policy visits each state:

```text
r(π) = Σ_s  μ_π(s) * r(s, π(s))
```

where `μ_π(s)` is the **stationary distribution** of states under `π` (how often the chain is in `s` per step), and `r(s, π(s))` is the expected immediate reward in that state under the policy.

### Why This Is Useful

- **No discount tuning.** No more debating whether `γ = 0.84` or `0.99` is "right."
- **Fair to near and far rewards.** The agent values a reward one step away the same as a reward one million steps away.
- **Clean objective.** Maximize a single rate instead of a sum that could diverge.

> **Analogy:** If you run a lemonade stand, you don't care *when* during the day the customers show up — you care about dollars per hour. Average reward is that rate-of-earning.

---

## Differential Return

Once `r(π)` is defined, define the **differential return** starting from time `t`:

```text
G_t  =  Σ_{k=0..∞}  ( R_{t+k+1}  −  r(π) )
```

This is the sum of *surprise* rewards — how much each reward **beats** (`+`) or **falls short of** (`−`) the agent's average.

> **Analogy:** If your average wage is $20/hour, an hour that pays $25 contributes `+$5` to your differential "surprise" for that hour; an hour that pays $15 contributes `−$5`. Differential return is the running tally of beating-or-lagging your average.

The expected value of `G_t` is zero when starting from the stationary distribution — the good and bad surprises cancel out in the long run.

---

## Differential Value Functions

Differential value functions measure the expected differential return from a state (or state-action pair):

```text
v_π(s) = E_π[ G_t  |  S_t = s ]
q_π(s, a) = E_π[ G_t  |  S_t = s, A_t = a ]
```

These obey **differential Bellman equations** analogous to the discounted ones, but with `r(π)` playing the role of a "subtracted baseline":

```text
v_π(s) = Σ_a π(a|s) Σ_{s', r} p(s', r | s, a) [ r − r(π) + v_π(s') ]
q_π(s, a) = Σ_{s', r} p(s', r | s, a) [ r − r(π) + Σ_{a'} π(a'|s') q_π(s', a') ]
```

Subtracting `r(π)` keeps the values bounded even when the horizon is infinite.

### Differential Semi-gradient Sarsa

To learn `q̂ ≈ q*`, update after each step:

```text
R̄  ←  R̄ + α * (R − R̄)                          # running estimate of r(π)
q̂(S, A)  ←  q̂(S, A) + α * ( R − R̄ + q̂(S', A') − q̂(S, A) )
```

`R̄` is the empirical average reward — a moving target that the algorithm tracks while it learns the action values. This is the **average-reward analogue** of tabular semi-gradient Sarsa.

## Advantages and Disadvantages

### Advantages

- **Simplicity.** The objective is one number — easy to compare policies directly.
- **Stationarity-friendly.** No need to know episode length; works naturally for continuing tasks.
- **Clear performance metric.** Algorithms can optimize it directly.
- **Discount-free.** No `γ` tuning drama, no risk of `γ = 1` blow-up.

### Disadvantages

- **Ignores timing of rewards.** All steps count equally, so an algorithm can favor steady-state policies even when a delayed big reward would be preferable in a finite-horizon task.
- **Convergence challenges.** Estimating `r(π)` while simultaneously learning values introduces moving-target instability, especially in non-stationary or complex dynamics.
- **Variance.** Differences `R_{t+1} − r(π)` can be noisy, slowing learning compared to discounted targets.

---

## When to Use Average Reward

Use average reward when:
- The task is **continuing** (no natural episodes or resets).
- You care about **long-run throughput** (queueing, control, robotics running forever).
- Episode length is unknown or varies.

Stick with **discounted** RL when:
- Episodes are short and well-defined.
- Far-future rewards should genuinely be worth less (e.g., economic discounting).
- You need the mathematical convenience of contraction mappings under `γ < 1`.

---

## Key Takeaways

- The **average reward** `r(π) = lim E[(1/T) Σ R_t]` is the long-run *rate* of reward per step, sidestepping the need to tune a discount factor in continuing tasks.
- It can also be written as `r(π) = Σ μ_π(s) r(s, π(s))`, an expectation over the stationary distribution.
- The **differential return** `G_t = Σ (R_{t+k+1} − r(π))` measures above/below-average rewards, giving rise to **differential value functions** `v_π` and `q_π`.
- Differential Bellman equations look like the discounted ones with `r(π)` subtracted from each reward — keeping values bounded despite the infinite horizon.
- **Differential semi-gradient Sarsa** tracks `R̄` as a running estimate of `r(π)` and updates `q̂` using `R − R̄` as the TD target.
- Average reward is great for continuing tasks and discount-free objectives, but it loses information about reward timing and can be harder to make converge.
- Choose average reward when **rate** matters; choose discounted RL when **timing** matters.
