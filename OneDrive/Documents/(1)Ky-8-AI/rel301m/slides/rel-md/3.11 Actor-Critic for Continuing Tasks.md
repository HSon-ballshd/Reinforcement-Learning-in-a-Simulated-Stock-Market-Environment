# 3.11 Actor-Critic for Continuing Tasks

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this lecture, you should be able to:

- **Derive a sample-based estimate** for the policy gradient in continuing (average-reward) tasks.
- **Understand the Actor-Critic algorithm** — its two-network architecture, how the actor and critic interact, and how updates are computed.

---

## 1. Why We Need the Policy Gradient

In **continuing tasks** (no terminal state, infinite horizon), the natural objective is the **average reward per time-step**, not a discounted return that decays to zero. The gradient of this average reward w.r.t. policy parameters `θ` tells us which direction to nudge the policy to get better long-run performance.

### Gradient of the Average Reward

For a policy `π(a|s,θ)`, the gradient of the average reward `r(π)` from a single state `S_t` is:

```
∇ r(π)  ∝  E[ ∇log π(A|S,θ) · q_π(S,A) ]
```

Because we follow the policy, we observe states under the **on-policy distribution**, so we can simply **sample transitions while running the policy** and average:

```
∇ r(π)  ≈  (1/n) Σ_t  ∇log π(A_t|S_t,θ) · q_π(S_t, A_t)
```

This is an **unbiased sample-based gradient** — we don't need to enumerate states, we just let the policy visit them.

---

## 2. Ways to Estimate the Policy Gradient

The lecture sketches the family of estimators used in policy-based RL:

- **Finite Difference Methods** — perturb parameters slightly, measure change in return. Crude, high variance, but doesn't need a differentiable model.
- **Score Function Methods** — directly estimate the gradient via `∇log π(a|s,θ)` (the "log-derivative trick"). Foundation of REINFORCE-style algorithms.
- **Likelihood Ratio Methods** — score-function methods with a **baseline subtracted** from the return to reduce variance (without introducing bias).
- **Actor-Critic Methods** — combine a **likelihood-ratio (score-function) policy gradient** with a **learned value-function baseline (the critic)**. This is what this lecture builds toward.
- **Natural Policy Gradient** — premultiply the gradient by the **Fisher information matrix** to respect the geometry of the parameter space, giving faster and more stable convergence.

> **Analogy**: think of climbing a foggy mountain. Finite difference is tapping the ground with a stick; score function is following your compass; the baseline is a fellow hiker telling you "you're above average already"; actor-critic adds a **GPS device (the critic)** that gives more accurate elevation feedback; the natural gradient upgrades your compass to one that's calibrated to the mountain's curvature.

---

## 3. The Actor-Critic Architecture

Actor-critic sits in the middle of the RL method zoo: it is **policy-based** (because it has an explicit actor) but **value-aware** (because a critic supplies low-variance targets).

### Two Components

- **Actor** — the **policy** `π(a|s,θ)`. It chooses actions. Think of it as **the driver** holding the steering wheel.
- **Critic** — the **value function** `v̂(s,w)` (or `q̂(s,a,w)`), parameterised by `w`. It evaluates how good the actor's current behaviour is. Think of it as the **back-seat coach** whispering "you're doing well here, watch out there."

> **Joke**: actor-critic is the only marriage in ML where one partner (the critic) literally tells the other (the actor) what to do — and both get better over time.

### Why Two Networks?

The pure REINFORCE update uses the **Monte Carlo return** `G_t` as a noisy, high-variance estimate of `q_π(S_t, A_t)`. By replacing that noisy target with a **bootstrapped estimate from the critic**, we:

1. **Cut variance dramatically** (TD error has lower variance than full returns).
2. **Allow online, step-by-step updates** — no need to wait for an episode to end, which is essential for continuing tasks.
3. **Make the method compatible with function approximation** at scale.

---

## 4. Actor-Critic Algorithm — Step by Step

```
1. Initialise actor parameters θ and critic parameters w (small random values).
2. Repeat for each time-step t:
   a. Observe state S_t, sample action A_t ~ π(·|S_t, θ).
   b. Take action, observe reward R_{t+1} and next state S_{t+1}.
   c. Compute TD error:
        δ_t = R_{t+1} - r̄ + v̂(S_{t+1}, w) - v̂(S_t, w)
      (r̄ is the running estimate of average reward)
   d. Update CRITIC (e.g. semi-gradient TD(0)):
        w ← w + α_w · δ_t · ∇_w v̂(S_t, w)
   e. Update ACTOR (policy gradient with critic as baseline):
        θ ← θ + α_θ · δ_t · ∇_θ log π(A_t | S_t, θ)
3. Update r̄ ← r̄ + β · δ_t  (running average reward).
4. Continue indefinitely (this is a continuing task — there is no "done").
```

### The Key Insight: TD Error as Advantage

The **TD error** `δ_t` is a sample of the **advantage** `A_π(s,a) = q_π(s,a) - v_π(s)`:

```
δ_t  ≈  q_π(S_t, A_t) - v_π(S_t)
```

So in actor-critic, the same scalar `δ_t` is doing double duty:

- For the **critic**, it is the **TD learning target** (used to fit the value function).
- For the **actor**, it is an **advantage estimator** that tells the policy "this action was better (or worse) than the state's average."

This is the bridge between value-based and policy-based RL, and it's what makes actor-critic both **stable** and **principled**.

---

## 5. Compatible Function Approximation Theorem

A classic result (Sutton, McAllester, Singh, Mansour) says that if you use the **actor-critic architecture**, the critic's value function is a **compatible function approximator** when:

- It is **linear** in features `∇_θ log π(a|s,θ)`.
- Its weights `w` are learned to **minimise the mean-squared error** between `v̂(s,w)` and the true `q_π(s,a)` (or the advantage).

Under these conditions, the actor-critic update — using `δ_t` as the advantage estimate — is **guaranteed to follow the true policy gradient** with **no asymptotic bias**, even when the actor's policy is non-linear (e.g., a neural network). This is the theoretical safety net behind the algorithm.

In exam language:

> *"If the critic's approximator is compatible, the actor-critic update is unbiased and converges to a local optimum of the average reward."*

## 6. Real-Life Example: A Self-Driving Car

- **Actor** = the controller that decides steering angle and acceleration from camera inputs.
- **Critic** = a learned model that predicts the next few seconds' reward (stay in lane, reach goal, avoid collision).
- At every tick, the **TD error** signals "this lane-change was better (or worse) than average."
- The critic updates its value estimates; the actor updates its policy proportionally to the TD error times the gradient of `log π`.

Over millions of miles, the driver gets smoother, and the back-seat coach gets sharper — the classic actor-critic virtuous cycle.

## 7. Quick Pseudocode Recap

```
# One-step actor-critic for continuing tasks
Input: π(a|s,θ), v̂(s,w), step-sizes α_θ, α_w, β
Initialise θ, w, r̄ = 0
Loop forever:
    S ← current state
    A ~ π(·|S,θ)
    Take A, observe R, S'
    δ ← R - r̄ + v̂(S', w) - v̂(S, w)
    w ← w + α_w · δ · ∇v̂(S, w)        # critic update
    θ ← θ + α_θ · δ · ∇log π(A|S, θ)  # actor update
    r̄ ← r̄ + β · δ                      # average reward update
```

---

## Key Takeaways

- In **continuing tasks** the right objective is the **average reward**, and the policy gradient is the gradient of this average.
- The **policy gradient** can be estimated by sampling on-policy transitions and using the **log-derivative trick**.
- **Actor-critic = actor (policy) + critic (value function)** — combining policy-based and value-based RL.
- The **TD error `δ_t`** serves as a low-variance **advantage estimator** that drives both updates: critic fits `v̂` to minimise `δ²`; actor follows `Δθ = α·δ·∇log π`.
- A running **average-reward estimate `r̄`** replaces the discount factor; the critic uses `R - r̄ + v̂(S')` as its bootstrapped target.
- The **Compatible Function Approximation Theorem** guarantees that with a linear critic on the right features, the actor-critic update is **unbiased**.
- Compared to REINFORCE, actor-critic trades a little bias for **much lower variance** and works **online, step-by-step** — essential for continuing problems.
