# 2.4 Off-Policy Learning for Prediction

## Why Off-Policy?

In **on-policy** learning, the policy you use to *act* is the same one you *evaluate and improve*. In **off-policy** learning, the agent learns about a **target policy** `π` while actually behaving according to a different **behavior policy** `b` (sometimes called `μ` in the textbook).

- **Behavior policy** `b`: the policy that *generates* the data. It selects actions the agent actually takes.
- **Target policy** `π`: the policy being *learned or evaluated* — e.g., the optimal policy.

> Real-life analogy: think of a **market researcher** (target policy, the decision-maker they want to model) studying shoppers by watching someone else's **focus group** (behavior policy). They re-weight what they see to estimate the behavior of their target audience.

### Why bother?

- The target policy (e.g., the optimal one) might be **deterministic and risk-averse**, so if the agent followed it directly it would only visit a tiny slice of the state space.
- A behavior policy that favors **exploration** — e.g., a uniform random policy — visits far more states.
- Off-policy lets us learn the value of `π` from the rich experience collected under `b`.

### The Coverage Rule

A necessary condition for off-policy learning:

> If `π(a|s) > 0` for some action `a` in state `s`, then we must have `b(a|s) > 0` as well.

In words: **the behavior policy must cover the target policy** — every action the target considers possible must also be possible under the behavior. Otherwise there are transitions the data simply cannot inform us about.

Note: off-policy is a **strict generalization** of on-policy. On-policy is just the special case where `π = b`.

## Importance Sampling — The Core Idea

We have samples drawn from distribution `b`, but we want an expectation under a different distribution `π`. A plain sample average gives us `E_b[x]`, not `E_π[x]`.

The trick: **reweight each sample** by how much more (or less) likely it is under `π` than under `b`.

### The Importance Sampling Ratio

For a single sample `x`, the ratio is:

```
ρ(x) = π(x) / b(x)
```

If `π` thinks `x` is twice as likely as `b` does, that sample gets weight 2 — it's effectively counted twice. If `π` thinks `x` is half as likely, it gets weight 1/2.

> Analogy: imagine a pollster who surveyed Republicans (behavior distribution) but wants to estimate the overall national vote (target distribution). They **up-weight** Democrat-heavy districts and **down-weight** deep-red ones so the weighted average mirrors the electorate. That's importance sampling.

### Weighted Average Estimator

To estimate `E_π[x]` from samples `x_1, ..., x_n` drawn from `b`:

```
E_π[x] ≈ (1/n) * Σ ρ(x_i) * x_i     where ρ(x_i) = π(x_i) / b(x_i)
```

Each sample is multiplied by its importance ratio, then averaged.

---

## Importance Sampling for Trajectories

In RL we don't have independent samples — we have **trajectories** (sequences of state-action-reward-next-state). The ratio extends to a whole trajectory from step `t` to terminal step `T`:

```
ρ_{t:T-1} = Π_{k=t}^{T-1}  π(A_k | S_k) / b(A_k | S_k)
```

We multiply the per-step ratios along the trajectory because the probability of a whole trajectory factors as a product of conditional action probabilities (assuming Markovian dynamics).

This trajectory-level ratio becomes the weight applied to that trajectory's return when estimating the value of the target policy.

### Ordinary vs Weighted Importance Sampling

Two common estimators you should know for the exam:

**Ordinary importance sampling** — average the weighted returns:
```
V_π(s) ≈ Σ_n ρ_n * G_n / N
```

**Weighted importance sampling** — normalize by the sum of weights (lower variance, biased):
```
V_π(s) ≈ Σ_n ρ_n * G_n  /  Σ_n ρ_n
```

- **Ordinary IS** is unbiased but can have *very high variance* (a single unlikely trajectory with huge ρ can dominate).
- **Weighted IS** dramatically reduces variance at the cost of a small bias, and is usually preferred in practice for off-policy Monte Carlo prediction.

## Worked Intuition (from the slides)

The slides walk through a tiny numerical example: drawing `x` from distribution `b` and tracking the running estimate of `E_π[x]`.

- True expected value under `π` sits in the middle of a range.
- Each new sample `x ~ b` produces a new estimate that bounces around the true value.
- Over many samples the weighted average converges to `E_π[x]`, even though the raw samples never came from `π`.

Key takeaway from the example: the estimator is **consistent** — it converges to the right answer given enough samples, even with the "wrong" sampling distribution.

## Off-Policy Monte Carlo Prediction (Sketch)

For *every-visit* off-policy MC prediction of `V_π`:

1. Collect episodes by following behavior policy `b` (with coverage of `π`).
2. For each episode, compute the trajectory return `G_t` from time `t`.
3. Compute the trajectory importance ratio `ρ_{t:T-1}`.
4. Update the running estimate of `V_π(s)` using ordinary or weighted IS as above.

For *first-visit* MC, apply the same idea only to the first occurrence of `s` in each episode.

---

## Common Pitfalls

- **Forgetting coverage**: if `b(a|s) = 0` for some action `π` cares about, the ratio is undefined (or zero where it shouldn't be).
- **Ignoring variance**: ordinary IS estimates can explode when a long trajectory contains a rare action under `b`. Weighted IS or truncation/capping of ratios is often needed.
- **Conflating on- and off-policy**: on-policy assumes `π = b`, so all ratios are 1. Off-policy earns its power only when the two policies actually differ.

---

## Key Takeaways

- **Off-policy learning** evaluates/improves a *target policy* `π` using data from a different *behavior policy* `b`.
- The behavior policy must **cover** the target: `π(a|s) > 0 ⇒ b(a|s) > 0`.
- **Importance sampling** corrects the mismatch by weighting each sample by `ρ = π / b`.
- For RL trajectories, the ratio is a **product over time**: `ρ_{t:T-1} = Π π(A_k|S_k) / b(A_k|S_k)`.
- **Ordinary IS** is unbiased but high variance; **weighted IS** has lower variance and is usually preferred.
- Off-policy is a strict generalization of on-policy (`π = b` recovers the on-policy case).
- Importance sampling is what lets Monte Carlo methods learn the value of an optimal/deterministic policy from exploratory data.
