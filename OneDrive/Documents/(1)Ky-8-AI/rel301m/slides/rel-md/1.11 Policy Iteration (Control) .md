# 1.11 Policy Iteration (Control)

## Overview

Policy iteration is the classic **control algorithm** in reinforcement learning: given an MDP with known dynamics, it computes the **optimal policy** and **optimal value function**. The intuition is simple — keep alternating between "rate your current strategy" and "switch to a better strategy based on that rating" until nothing changes.

> Analogy: imagine trying every restaurant in town. After each visit you have a "rating" (value function). Next time, you **greedily** go to the highest-rated one. Eventually, no new restaurant beats your current favourite — you've converged on the optimal choice.

The deck's objectives:
- Understand the **policy improvement theorem**.
- Use a value function for a policy to produce a **better policy** for a given MDP.
- Apply **policy iteration** to compute optimal policies and optimal value functions.

---

## The Policy Improvement Idea

Suppose you already have a policy `π` and you know its value function `v_π(s)` — i.e. "if I start in state `s` and follow `π`, how much reward do I expect?". You can ask a sharper question: **what is the value of taking action `a` now, then following `π` afterwards**?

This is the **action-value** under `π`:

```
q_π(s, a) = Σ_{s', r} p(s', r | s, a) [ r + γ v_π(s') ]
```

Now, why follow `π` at all in state `s`? Just pick the action whose `q_π(s, a)` is largest. That's the **greedy policy**:

```
π'(s) = argmax_a Σ_{s', r} p(s', r | s, a) [ r + γ v_π(s') ]
        = argmax_a q_π(s, a)
```

This is exactly what the **Bellman optimality equation** prescribes for each state.

---

## Policy Improvement Theorem

The key theoretical guarantee that makes the whole algorithm work.

**Statement.** Let `π` and `π'` be two deterministic policies such that for every state `s`:

```
q_π(s, π'(s)) ≥ v_π(s)
```

Then `π'` is at least as good as `π`:

```
v_{π'}(s) ≥ v_π(s)    for all s
```

Moreover, if the inequality is **strict in at least one state**, then `π'` is **strictly better** than `π` (improves the value in that state, and possibly others).

**Why this matters.** Because `π'` is greedy w.r.t. `q_π`, the inequality holds by construction — so `π'` is guaranteed to be no worse than `π`. If `π` was already optimal, the greedy policy equals `π` and the algorithm stops.

> Joke: "Try a strategy, see how good it is, then pick the best action next time" — that one-line summary *is* the policy improvement theorem in plain English.

## The 4×4 Gridworld Example

The deck walks through the standard 4×4 gridworld from Sutton & Barto:
- Each step gives reward `-1` until the terminal state is reached.
- Start with a uniform random policy `π_1`.
- Iterative policy evaluation yields the final value function `v_{π_1}` — values are more negative the farther you are from the goal.
- The **greedy policy** `π` then picks, in each cell, the move that maximises `q_{π_1}(s, a)` — this is a deterministic policy that heads toward the goal.

The gridworld is a useful concrete check: you can compute `v` by hand for small cases and watch the greedy policy sharpen as `v` becomes more accurate.

## The Two-Step Policy Iteration Algorithm

Policy iteration alternates between two distinct steps:

### Step 1 — Policy Evaluation
Given a policy `π_k`, compute its value function `v_{π_k}`. In the exact (tabular) version this means solving the linear system or running iterative policy evaluation until convergence.

### Step 2 — Policy Improvement
Greedify with respect to `v_{π_k}`:

```
π_{k+1}(s) = argmax_a Σ_{s', r} p(s', r | s, a) [ r + γ v_{π_k}(s') ]
```

This produces a new, deterministic policy `π_{k+1}` that is guaranteed to be at least as good as `π_k`.

### Pseudocode

```
Initialize π_0 arbitrarily
repeat
    # --- Evaluation ---
    v ← v_π               # compute value of current policy
    # --- Improvement ---
    π_new(s) ← argmax_a Σ_{s',r} p(s',r|s,a)[ r + γ v(s') ]   for all s
until π_new == π
return π, v
```

### Why both steps?

- After the **improvement** step, `π_{k+1}` is greedy w.r.t. `v_{π_k}`, but `v_{π_k}` no longer accurately reflects `v_{π_{k+1}}`.
- After the **evaluation** step, `v_{π_k}` accurately reflects `π_k`, but `π_k` may not be optimal yet.
- When both are true simultaneously — **policy is greedy and the value function is accurate** — Bellman's optimality equation is satisfied, and we have the optimal policy.

---

## Convergence Guarantee

Policy iteration converges in a **finite number of iterations** in the tabular setting:
1. Each improvement step produces a strictly better policy (or leaves it unchanged).
2. There are only finitely many deterministic policies on a finite state-action MDP (`|A|^|S|`).
3. Therefore the sequence `π_1, π_2, π_3, ...` cannot cycle and must terminate.
4. **Termination condition**: the policy is unchanged between two iterations → the current policy is greedy w.r.t. its own value function → it satisfies the Bellman optimality equation → it is **optimal**.

In practice, convergence is extremely fast — typically only a handful of iterations, even on problems with millions of states.

---

## Properties of Policy Iteration

- **Deterministic policies**: each `π_k` produced by the greedy step is deterministic (ties broken arbitrarily).
- **Monotonic improvement**: `v_{π_{k+1}}(s) ≥ v_{π_k}(s)` for all `s, k`.
- **Exact (model-based)**: assumes full knowledge of `p(s', r | s, a)` and the reward function. This is the **planning** setting — not learning from samples.

> Real-life analogy: think of a chess engine that, after each full self-play simulation ("evaluation"), updates its opening repertoire to favour whatever opening produced the best expected outcome ("improvement"). It loops until the preferred opening stops changing.

## Connection to Iterative Policy Evaluation

The deck closes by emphasising that policy iteration **uses iterative policy evaluation** as a subroutine. So the tools covered earlier (Bellman expectation backup, in-place or synchronous sweeps, `θ`-threshold stopping) are exactly what powers the evaluation step here.

## Key Takeaways

- **Policy iteration = evaluation + improvement**, repeated until the policy stops changing.
- The **policy improvement theorem** guarantees that greedifying w.r.t. `q_π(s, a)` produces a policy no worse than `π` (strictly better unless `π` was already optimal).
- The greedy update is `π'(s) = argmax_a Σ_{s',r} p(s',r|s,a)[r + γ v_π(s')]`.
- After the **improvement** step, `π` is greedy but `v` is stale; after the **evaluation** step, `v` is accurate but `π` may be suboptimal. Optimality requires both.
- On finite MDPs, policy iteration converges in a **finite** number of iterations because there are only finitely many deterministic policies.
- Each iterated policy is **deterministic**; ties in the argmax can be broken arbitrarily.
- Policy iteration is a **model-based** planning algorithm — it requires known dynamics, unlike sample-based methods (Monte Carlo, TD) covered later.
